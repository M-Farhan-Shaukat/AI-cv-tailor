If you have ever applied for a job online and heard nothing back, there is a good chance a person never saw your CV. Most medium and large employers use an **applicant tracking system (ATS)** to collect, sort and filter applications before a recruiter reads them. Understanding how that software works is the easiest way to stop losing interviews you should have won.

## What an ATS actually does

An ATS is a database with a search engine on top. When you apply, it does three things:

1. **Parses your CV.** It pulls out your name, contact details, job titles, employers, dates, skills and education, and stores them in fields.
2. **Stores the application** against the job you applied for, so the recruiter sees every candidate in one list.
3. **Helps the recruiter filter and rank.** Recruiters search for keywords ("Laravel", "project management", "CPA"), filter by job title or location, and some systems rank candidates by how well they match the job.

The important point: an ATS rarely "rejects" a CV on its own. What happens is quieter. If your CV does not contain the words the recruiter searches for, or the parser cannot read it, you simply never appear near the top of the list.

## Why good candidates get filtered out

The most common reasons are not about experience at all:

- **Different wording.** The job asks for "customer success" and your CV says "client support". A human sees the match; a keyword search does not.
- **Skills hidden in the wrong place.** A skill that only appears in a sidebar or a graphic may not be parsed at all.
- **Layout the parser cannot read.** Tables, text boxes, columns and icons can scramble the order of your information or drop it entirely.
- **Missing basics.** No phone number, or contact details placed in the page header where some parsers do not look.

## How to work with an ATS, not against it

You do not need tricks. You need a CV that is easy to read and uses the job's language honestly.

- **Mirror the job description's wording** for skills you genuinely have. If they say "stakeholder management", use that phrase rather than a synonym.
- **Show keywords in context.** A skill listed in a skills section counts, but a skill used in a real achievement ("Built REST APIs in Laravel for three products") is stronger evidence for both the ATS and the recruiter.
- **Use standard headings:** Summary, Experience, Skills, Education. Creative headings can confuse parsers.
- **Keep the layout simple:** one column, real text, common fonts, no tables or images.
- **Include the exact job title** you are applying for in your headline or summary when it fits your background.

## Is "beating the ATS" honest?

Yes, as long as everything on your CV is true. An ATS is a search tool. Using the same words as the job description for skills you really have is simply making sure the search finds you. Adding skills you do not have might get you past a filter, but it will not get you past the interview.
